These are the words the rest of the documentation uses. Each one has a rule
behind it, and each rule has a reason.

## The agent

The agent is the Seyd program on the robot. It owns everything between a
configuration and a running robot: sockets, address discovery, the
certificate, the QUIC endpoint, the sessions, the signalling connection to the
cloud, and the maintenance that keeps all of it alive. In `seydd` and in every
SDK it is the same Rust type, `seyd_core::Agent`; the daemon and the SDKs are
hosts that supply media and consume its events.

<Figure caption="The agent lifecycle. Channels are declared before start; sessions come and go while it runs; stop tears everything down."><Lifecycle /></Figure>

The lifecycle is the same in every form factor: create the agent, add
channels, start, serve sessions, stop. Channels are added before start and
not after, because the robot announces its channel list to the cloud and the
pilot reads it from the first message of every session.

## Channels

A channel is one named stream with a kind, a direction and a codec. There are
three kinds.

<Figure caption="Video and sensor channels flow from the robot to every pilot. Command channels flow from the driver to the robot; the robot drops commands from observers."><Channels /></Figure>

| Kind | Direction | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| `video` | robot to pilot | Encoded access units, chunked into FEC blocks |
| `sensor` | robot to pilot | One message per datagram |
| `command` | pilot to robot | One message per datagram, from the driver only |

Channels are numbered from 1 in the order they are declared; channel 0 is the
control stream and never appears in a datagram. A channel's `codec` is a
WebCodecs identifier (`avc1.42001f`, `hev1.…`, `mjpeg`, or `json` for
messages) and it travels unchanged to the browser's decoder, which is why the
value has to be right on the robot side.

## Sessions and roles

A session is one pilot connected to one robot. The cloud assigns roles: the
first pilot to connect becomes the **driver**, and every later pilot is an
**observer**, up to the robot's `max_sessions`. Observers see the same video
and sensors and can send nothing. The role is fixed when the session is
offered and repeated to the robot, and the robot itself drops commands from
observers, so a page bug cannot make an observer drive. When the driver
leaves, observers are not promoted; the next new connection takes the driver
slot.

Every session is authorized by a short-lived token the pilot presents in its
first message, and the robot verifies it against the cloud's published key.
Your robot-side code learns about sessions through events (`on_session_ended`
in the SDKs, the `session` message on the daemon's publisher-control port), and
should park actuators when the last driver leaves, because that event fires
whether the pilot said goodbye or simply vanished.

## QoS profiles are ceilings

The robot picks one of three profiles, `latency`, `balanced` or `quality`,
and a driver may ask for another during a session. A profile is a ceiling,
not a setting: it states the highest bitrate the publisher may use, the
latency budget, the longest GOP, and Seyd's own FEC rates, drop thresholds and
presentation delay. The closed-loop controller moves the actual bitrate
request and the FEC rates inside that ceiling as the link changes, never
above it.

Seyd tells your encoder the targets through `video-config` and leaves
resolution, preset and VBV to you. Degrade resolution first and frame rate
last: for a remote pilot the interval between frames is latency, not quality.
The numbers for each profile are in
[QoS profiles](/docs/reference/qos-profiles/), rendered from the constants in
the code.

## Keyframes on demand

Seyd asks for a recovery point when a pilot needs one: on join, on loss it
could not repair, after a drop, on a stream switch. The request arrives at
your publisher as `recovery-request` with a `kind` of `ltr`, `intra_refresh`
or `idr`, cheapest first, and it is rate-limited to one per 250 ms per
channel. The profile's `maxGopMs` is only a safety net, the longest the
publisher may go without a full recovery point: 10 s on `latency` and
`balanced`, 4 s on `quality`.

The rule exists because a periodic IDR is several times the size of a delta
frame and lands in one frame slot, so a one-second IDR cadence is the latency
tail, the once-a-second hitch and a large share of the bandwidth, all for
recovery points nobody asked for. Prefer periodic intra refresh where the
encoder has it; otherwise a long GOP and an IDR on request.
[ADR 0009](/docs/reference/adr/0009-keyframes-on-demand/) has the
measurements; [The publisher contract](/docs/robot/publisher-contract/) has
what to implement.

## Whole frame or nothing

A frame is admitted before its first chunk leaves and is then sent in full. If
the send backlog is already longer than the profile allows, the next delta
frame is skipped whole rather than started and abandoned. A torn frame costs
a GOP of smear; a skipped frame costs one frame interval. Keyframes are never
dropped.

## FEC per block

Every video frame is sent as one or more blocks of up to 8 data chunks, and
each block carries its own Reed-Solomon parity chunks computed over that block
alone. The pilot rebuilds a block from any sufficient subset on arrival, with
no round trip. The profile sets how much parity a delta frame and a keyframe
get on a clean link, and the controller raises it under loss. What FEC cannot
repair is reported to the robot immediately and answered with a recovery
point.

## Presentation on the capture clock

Each frame carries the encoder's capture timestamp. The pilot decodes on
arrival but paints each frame at its capture slot plus a fixed presentation
delay from the profile, 50 to 150 ms, so the picture keeps the camera's
cadence and never drifts further behind. The budget is fixed on purpose: a
jitter buffer that grows to look smooth means acting on a world that has moved
on. Decoding and pacing run in a Web Worker on an OffscreenCanvas, so your
page cannot stutter the video by being busy. The decision is
[ADR 0005](/docs/reference/adr/0005-presentation-pacing/); what the HUD's
numbers mean is in [Latency and the HUD](/docs/pilot/latency/).

## The direct path and the relay

The robot announces every address it has, the cloud probes the public ones,
and the pilot races them all. The winning candidate is shown in the HUD as the
path. If no candidate connects, the pilot reports what it found and what to
change, and, only if the robot allows it, attaches to the cloud relay for this
session. A relayed session is marked in amber on every surface for as long as
it lasts, and there is no live upgrade back to a direct path: reload to
retry. See [Reachability](/docs/networking/reachability/).

## Three identity planes

Seyd has three kinds of identity and only one of them touches an identity
provider.

| Plane | Who | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Robot identity | The robot | An Ed25519 key generated on the robot, registered once by enrolment, proven by challenge-response on every connection |
| Session authorization | A pilot in one session | An ES256 token minted by the cloud with Seyd's own key, valid for at most five minutes, verified by the robot |
| Human identity | A person in the console | OIDC sign-in through the configured provider, behind one seam in the cloud |

Robots and sessions never depend on the provider, so a fleet keeps working
while the identity provider is down or being replaced. Who may drive which
robot is not a provider question either: organisations, roles and robot
grants live in Seyd's own database.
[Enrolment and access](/docs/cloud/access/) covers all three;
[ADR 0007](/docs/reference/adr/0007-identity/) is the decision.
