The problem

The internet was not built for driving things.

Every team hits the same three walls: the picture lags, it smears the moment the robot moves, and the robot cannot be reached from outside its own network. The familiar tools were built for other jobs.

Familiar toolBuilt forWhy it breaks down for driving
SSH tunnelsFiles and terminals"Reliable" means it stops and re-sends whenever data goes missing. Every loss becomes a freeze.
Remote desktopVNC, RDPViewing a screenThe feed is decoded, redrawn and re-compressed before it ships. Delay and quality lost at every step.
VPNsWireGuard, TailscaleJoining two networksA tunnel moves bytes. It cannot tell a frame from a steering command, or ask your camera to adapt.
Video-call techWebRTC platformsSmooth conversationBuffers on purpose to look smooth. Harmless in a meeting; undriveable in a vehicle.

The pattern

All of them treat your data as anonymous bytes on a reliable pipe. Driving needs the opposite: a system that knows a video frame from a steering command, and what to drop when the network has a bad second.

The honest version

On a perfect wired LAN a VPN gets close. Robots live behind 4G routers, satellite dishes and factory firewalls.

How it works

Our cloud makes the introduction. Then it gets out of the way.

A small program on the robot, one element in the operator's web page, and a signalling cloud that brokers a direct, encrypted connection between them. Video, sensors and commands never pass through our servers.

Your video, untouched

We forward your camera's own encoded bytes. Never decoded, re-compressed or stored. Quality is whatever your camera produces; privacy is structural, not a policy.

One driver, many watchers

One click takes the wheel; colleagues watch the same feed read-only. The robot itself enforces a single driver, and the console shows who has it.

Honest when it cannot connect

If a network blocks direct connections, Seyd names what it found and the setting to change. If you allow it, our relay carries the session as a last resort, and every screen says so.

Latency

Delay is a stack of small numbers. We removed the two biggest.

Video-call technology re-compresses your video on a server and holds frames in a buffer that grows so playback looks smooth. Both exist for someone else's problem. Seyd does neither.

What remains is your camera's encoder, the browser's decoder, the speed of light, and a presentation budget you set. Frames are paced on the camera's clock inside that budget, so the picture is steady and never drifts further behind.

A growing buffer is the enemy

A jitter buffer trades time for smoothness. Fine for a film. For a human steering two tonnes, it means acting on a world that has moved on. Seyd's budget is fixed, 50 to 150 ms by profile, and never grows to hide a bad second.

Off the main thread

Decoding and pacing run in a worker. Your web app cannot stutter the video by being busy.

Loss

Mobile networks have a bad second every minute. We plan for it.

You cannot stop a network from losing packets. You can decide what happens when it does.

  • Never fall behind. On a hiccup Seyd skips a frame instead of adding delay. The operator always sees now.
  • Repair without a round trip. Every frame carries spare Reed-Solomon material, so most losses are rebuilt on arrival.
  • Whole frame or nothing. A frame is admitted before its first byte leaves. A torn frame costs a second of smear; a skipped one costs a fortieth.
  • Adapt at the source. Seyd measures the link and asks the camera for less when it narrows, more when it recovers. Where the camera offers several streams, Seyd switches instead of re-encoding. Resolution first, frame rate last: frame interval is latency.
  • Never lie to the operator. If the picture cannot be trusted, the screen says so and a clean frame is requested at once.

What the repair is worth

At 5% packet loss, 99.3% of frames still arrive complete. Without repair material, 71.9% do, and every damaged frame smears the ones after it.

Reachability

The hard part is not the video. It is reaching the robot.

Robots sit behind 4G routers, satellite dishes and factory firewalls, networks built to keep connections out. Seyd works the problem before anyone tries to connect.

Known before anyone connects

The console shows each robot's reachability up front: direct connection likely, or this site needs a change, with the change named.

Typical deployments

Office or depot Wi-Fi: works out of the box. 4G/5G routers: enable UPnP or NAT-PMP and Seyd opens the pinhole itself, or forward one UDP port once. Starlink: a documented configuration.

relayed

When direct is impossible

The direct race always runs first. Only when it fails, and the robot permits it, does the session go through our relay. The HUD, the status line and the guidance box all say so, and why. Metered, priced separately, never hidden.

Integration

Point Seyd at what you already have.

Seyd does not replace your cameras, your autonomy stack or your safety logic. It states its needs; your systems decide how to meet them. A first integration is an afternoon.

On the robot: a short configuration file

# /etc/seyd/seydd.toml — the daemon needs no code
[[channel]]
kind  = "video"
name  = "main"
input = "rtsp://192.168.1.20:554/Streaming/Channels/101"
codec = "avc1.42001f"          # or rtp://0.0.0.0:5000

[[channel]]
kind  = "sensor"
name  = "telemetry"
input = "udp://127.0.0.1:5002"

[[channel]]
kind   = "command"
name   = "drive"
output = "udp://127.0.0.1:5004"

The daemon runs on the robot's own Linux computer, reads the stream the camera already produces and hands commands to your control software as UDP datagrams. It tells your systems when the camera should adjust and when an operator connects or leaves.

On the operator side: one element on a web page

<script type="module">import '@seyd/web';</script>

<seyd-video robot-id="lift-truck-07"></seyd-video>
<seyd-hud></seyd-hud>

A live, drivable feed inside your own web app: your branding, your workflow. Connection health, a "someone else is driving" indicator and the relayed-session notice come with it. A React wrapper is included.

Or the engine directly

seyd_agent_create(&cfg, &callbacks, &agent);
seyd_push_frame(agent, video, au, len, keyframe, capture_ts_us);

One Rust core with a C ABI. The daemon, Python, C++ and ROS 2 are thin wrappers with no protocol logic, so nothing drifts.

What the span covers

"Robot → browser" runs from the first byte leaving the robot's agent to the whole frame reassembled in the browser, measured on one clock. It excludes the camera's encoder and the presentation budget you set. Every number we quote says which span it covers.

Cloud and identity

Three parties in every session. Only one is ours.

Our cloud introduces robot and browser, decides who may connect to what, hands out short-lived passes, and records who had control when. Then it steps aside.

Robots keep working when sign-in is down

A robot proves itself with a key it generated and never shares. A driving session is a five-minute pass signed by our key. Only a human sign-in touches an identity provider, so a fleet keeps running while that provider is out.

Permissions are the product

No identity provider can say "Anna may drive robot 42 but only watch robot 7", so organisations, roles, grants and the audit log are ours, in Postgres. After any incident the first question is who had control at 14:32. We record it as a matter of course.

Portable by construction, in the EU today

Plain containers on Postgres. The whole cloud runs from one docker compose up on a laptop or inside your own building, with no hyperscaler dependency in application code. The public service runs in Belgium, its database in Frankfurt. Console access is by invitation.

Software you can read

Everything that runs on your hardware is open source: the engine, the daemon, every wrapper, the browser components. No held-back "smart part"; the value is the field-tuned system, not a withheld algorithm. Your engineers and auditors read every line inside your vehicle, and the protocol is public.

Why the model works

Your video never crosses our servers on a direct session, so our cost does not grow with your usage. Per robot we see a 100-byte heartbeat every five seconds. The one thing with real marginal cost, the relay, is metered and priced separately, never a silent fallback.
